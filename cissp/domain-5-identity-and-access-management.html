Identity and Access Management (IAM)

CISSP Domain Review · Ioannis Alexander Konstas

Who is this, is that really them, and what are they allowed to touch — and the machinery that keeps those answers correct as people join, change roles, and leave.

AAA

  • Identification — claiming an identity (a username).
  • Authentication — proving that claim (a password, token, biometric).
  • Authorization — what the now-verified identity is permitted to do.
  • Accounting/auditing — logging what was actually done, tying back to the identity (this is what makes non-repudiation enforceable in practice).

Authentication factors

  • Something you know — password, PIN.
  • Something you have — hardware token, smart card, phone (OTP app).
  • Something you are — biometrics (fingerprint, iris, gait). Measured by FRR (False Rejection Rate, Type I), FAR (False Acceptance Rate, Type II), and the CER/EER (Crossover/Equal Error Rate) where they meet — the lower the CER, the better the sensor.
  • Somewhere you are / something you do — location-based and behavioral factors, increasingly used as additional (not sole) signals.

MFA requires factors from at least two different categories — a password plus a PIN is still one factor (both "something you know").

Access control models

  • DAC (Discretionary) — the resource owner sets permissions (classic filesystem ACLs). Flexible, weak central control.
  • MAC (Mandatory) — a central authority sets access based on classification/clearance labels; users can't override it (maps directly to Bell-LaPadula/Biba).
  • RBAC (Role-Based) — permissions attach to roles, users are assigned roles. The dominant enterprise model — simplifies onboarding/offboarding and audit.
  • ABAC (Attribute-Based) — access decided dynamically from attributes of subject, object, and environment (e.g. "finance staff, from a managed device, during business hours"). The most granular, and the model zero-trust architectures lean on.
  • Rule-based — static if/then rules, often layered on top of another model (e.g. firewall ACLs).

Identity lifecycle

Provisioning (account creation, tied to HR onboarding) → access reviews/recertification (periodic, catches privilege creep) → modification (role change should trigger both grants and revocations) → deprovisioning (must be prompt on termination — a classic audit finding is orphaned accounts from employees who left months ago). Privilege creep is the accumulation of unneeded access as someone changes roles without old access being revoked; periodic entitlement review is the control that catches it.

Federation and modern auth

  • SSO — one authentication event grants access across multiple systems. Improves usability and reduces password fatigue, but raises the blast radius of a single compromised credential.
  • SAML — XML-based, common for enterprise SSO to web apps; roles are Identity Provider (IdP) and Service Provider (SP).
  • OAuth 2.0 — authorization framework (delegates access to a resource, e.g. "let App X read my calendar") — not itself an authentication protocol.
  • OpenID Connect (OIDC) — an authentication layer built on top of OAuth 2.0; this is the piece that actually answers "who is this user."
  • Kerberos — ticket-based authentication (KDC issues a TGT, then service tickets); vulnerable if the KDC is compromised (single point of failure) and to replay if clocks drift too far.

Privileged access management

Admin/root accounts get extra controls beyond normal IAM: just-in-time elevation instead of standing privilege, session recording, credential vaulting/rotation, and separate break-glass emergency accounts — because a compromised privileged account is the fastest path to full domain compromise.
